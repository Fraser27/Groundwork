How it works

The predicate vocabulary

A predicate is the middle of a fact: the relationship in "counsel represents party". Groundwork keeps two vocabularies of them, one closed and one open, and the test for which list a relationship belongs in is a single question about consequences.

src/ontology/loader.py · ontologies/legal.yaml

Two tiers

Every fact in the graph is three parts: a subject, a predicate, an object. Counsel Smith — represents — Acme Corp. The predicate is the relationship name, and how strictly it is controlled turns out to matter a great deal.

Governing predicates

Closed list. Fourteen of them in the legal pack. These drive decisions with legal consequence: conflicts, privilege, deadlines, citation authority. A fact using a name outside the list is rejected when written.

Descriptive predicates

Open list. Subject-matter tags: what a document is about, what industry a party is in, what kind of deal something is. Sprawl here costs search precision, not a negligence claim.

The test for which tier a relationship belongs in, stated in the vocabulary file itself:

Would a wrong answer here embarrass you, or expose you? Embarrass → descriptive. Expose → governing.

The legal pack's governing list covers REPRESENTS, ADVERSE_TO, PARTY_TO, CITES, OVERRULES, DISTINGUISHES, FILED_IN, DEADLINE_FOR, SUBJECT_TO_PRIVILEGE, SUPERSEDES, GOVERNED_BY, CONTAINS_CLAUSE, RELATES_TO_MATTER and INSTRUCTS. Its descriptive list is CONCERNS_TOPIC, IN_INDUSTRY, HAS_DEAL_TYPE and MENTIONS.

A relationship name the vocabulary has never seen at all is treated as descriptive. That asymmetry is intentional: a new subject-matter tag should not require a vocabulary release, whereas a new governing relationship is a deliberate schema decision that somebody should have to make on purpose.

The failure the closed list prevents

This is not a data-quality annoyance. It is the single most instructive failure in the system, because the symptom is a clean-looking report.

The conflict check that returns nothing

An extractor reads a document and records that a firm acts for a company. It names the relationship is_counsel_to — a reasonable name, produced by a model that was not told otherwise, or by a developer who did not check.

Later, a conflict check runs. It asks the graph: who do we represent? It queries REPRESENTS.

Zero rows come back.

Now look at what that means from the outside. The check ran. It did not error. It returned a result. The result was empty. Every signal available to the person reading the report says the same thing an actually-clean conflict check would say: no conflicts found.

The firm proceeds. The conflict exists, is recorded in the graph, and was invisible to the query that was written to find it — because of a synonym.

This is not a bug that produces a wrong number. It is a bug that produces a reassuring number, and it is why the check is at write time.

The design conclusion follows directly: a write that fails loudly is enormously preferable to a query that silently returns nothing. So a governing relationship name is validated against the closed list at the moment a fact is created, and an unapproved name is refused with an error that explains what to do — map it to an approved relationship, or classify it as descriptive.

The same reasoning is why the descriptive list is open. If a document is tagged concerns_topic: shipping by one extractor and about_subject: shipping by another, search precision suffers. Somebody finds fewer relevant documents than they might have. That is a bad day. It is not a malpractice event, and paying the cost of a closed list — a vocabulary release for every new tag — to prevent it would be paying the wrong price.

Why grounding uses no language model

Grounding is the step that maps a proposed relationship name onto one the vocabulary knows. When a model returns acts_on_behalf_of, something has to decide whether that means REPRESENTS.

In Groundwork that decision is made by exact matching and nothing else. The proposed name is normalised — trimmed, upper-cased, spaces and hyphens turned into underscores — and compared against the vocabulary's identifiers and their display labels. A match returns the canonical name. No match returns nothing, and the claim is dropped.

So represents, REPRESENTS, Represents and adverse to all ground successfully. acts_on_behalf_of does not, and is discarded.

The rejected alternative

A more permissive mode exists in a comparable system: a language model adjudicates whether two relationship names mean the same thing, and maps the proposal onto the vocabulary accordingly. It would raise recall noticeably, and it was rejected.

The reason is that a model deciding acts_on_behalf_of means REPRESENTS is a schema decision made by a model. Schema decisions in a system that must be defensible belong to people, in a versioned file, reviewable by whoever will have to answer for them. A model that gets it right nine times in ten is a model that has silently redefined a governing relationship one time in ten, and there is no record of it having happened.

Losing recall to strictness is a visible cost — a claim was dropped, and the log says so. Gaining recall by letting a model edit the schema is an invisible cost. Between a visible cost and an invisible one, in this system, the visible one wins every time.

The consequence of strictness is that dropping claims is normal and expected. A model proposing an unrecognised relationship has its claim discarded and logged. That is the system working: no model output silently widens the vocabulary.

Vocabulary packs and domain-independence

The vocabulary lives in a file, not in code. A pack names the entity types of a domain, its two tiers of relationships, and its rules. The platform loads whichever pack it is pointed at, and the legal pack is simply the default.

A second pack for healthcare ships alongside it. Not because healthcare is a target market, but because a claim to be domain-agnostic that has only ever been tested against one domain is a claim that will fail the first time it is tested. If the second pack ever stops loading cleanly, the abstraction has rotted, and it is much better to learn that from a test than from a customer.

The healthcare pack applies the same two-tier test, with "expose" reinterpreted for the domain: there, it means patient harm or a regulatory finding, so contraindications and consent are governing. The entity types map recognisably onto the legal ones — a patient plays the role a party does, an encounter plays the role a matter does — which is a good sign that the abstraction is real rather than a legal model with the labels changed.

Rules live in the pack too

A pack can define rules that derive new facts from existing ones. Each rule names the pattern it fires on, the fact it produces, its own version, and — importantly — the minimum kind of belief its premises must have.

The legal pack defines two:

RuleFires whenProduces
conflict_check The firm represents a party, and a matter is adverse to that same party. A potential-conflict flag on that matter.
authority_stale A document cites an authority, and another authority overrules it. A flag that the document relies on stale authority.

Both require premises at least as solid as a confirmed extraction. The pack states why for the conflict check, and the reasoning generalises: a conflict flag resting on a language model's guess would be worse than none. Worse, not merely less useful — because a false conflict flag consumes senior time, may cause a firm to decline profitable work, and erodes trust in every other flag the system raises. The rule declines to fire rather than fire on a guess.

How the rule actually declines

Not by epistemic class, which is where the first attempt went wrong. A governing predicate may never be EXTRACTED_DET — a quote match proves text is present, never that it is significant — so a rule demanding that class as its floor could not have fired on anything read from a document at all. It would have declined always, and silently.

What the engine requires instead is that a premise has been signed off: approved by a person, or auto-asserted because a system of record declared it. A pack can loosen the class floor; no pack setting lets a rule fire on an unreviewed claim, on a rejected one, or on one since withdrawn.

Still not enforced anywhere: a relationship can be marked symmetric in the pack, and that flag is parsed and displayed but nothing acts on it.

The safe default

When an extractor sees two entities in the same passage but cannot justify anything stronger, the right answer is MENTIONS — a descriptive relationship recording that a name appears in the text, with no claim about what it was doing there.

This is the relationship a verified quote produces, and the only one it may produce. It is also the deliberate answer to a case where a stronger relationship looks free and is not: a case caption reading "Acme Corp v. Beta Holdings" proves both names appear. It does not establish, by mechanical means, that Acme is adverse to Beta — reading adversity out of a caption is interpretation, and interpretation disqualifies a claim from being treated as mechanically confirmed.

The consequence is honest and worth naming: adverse-party relationships do not come free from captions. If they are wanted, they belong to the model extractor with review, or to a rule with premises. That is a cost, and it is the correct cost. See How extraction works for the citation-parsing version of the same mistake, which is the one that actually shipped before being reversed.
