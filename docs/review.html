Using it

Reviewing claims

The review queue is the most important screen in the product, and its most important property is that it is short. A queue nobody can clear gets rubber-stamped, and a rubber-stamped queue destroys the guarantee it exists to provide.

src/documents/review.py · src/documents/retract.py

What is in the queue

Only claims where a human has something to contribute. In practice that means claims whose class is EXTRACTED_MODEL: things a model read into a document rather than merely found in it.

Not in the queue: facts a system of record declared, and quotes a search confirmed are on the page. Neither involves an unverified judgement, so there is nothing for a signature to add. Asking for one would produce a record saying a person checked something they did not meaningfully check.

The distinction is drawn on checkability, not on who proposed a claim — which is why a model's output can appear on both sides of it. See How extraction works.

Why it must stay small

This is the argument that shaped the extraction design, and it is worth following because the naive alternative sounds stricter and is in fact weaker.

Suppose every model output required review. A 300-page bundle produces hundreds of extracted claims. A single matter's ingest would produce a queue of that size, and several matters would produce a queue nobody could work through in a week.

What happens then is predictable and human: somebody selects all and approves. Not out of negligence — out of the reasonable judgement that the queue is an obstacle rather than a control, because a control you cannot exercise is an obstacle.

And at that moment every review record in the system becomes false. Each fact says a person approved it and names them. No person read it. The system will now print "approved by" alongside facts nobody looked at, and it will do so with complete confidence, to somebody relying on it.

The load-bearing claim

A review queue's value is not proportional to how much it contains. It is proportional to how much of it gets read. A queue holding ten genuine judgement calls, all read, is worth immeasurably more than a queue holding four hundred claims, bulk-approved — because the second one produces a false audit trail, and a false audit trail is worse than no audit trail.

So the queue holds judgement calls and nothing else. Everything mechanically checkable is checked mechanically, and the human is spent where a human is actually required.

What a reviewer sees

Each row carries what is needed to decide, and no more: the claim itself as subject, relationship and object; how it was reached and by which specific versioned producer; its confidence; the document and page; the quoted sentence; the matter; and when it was recorded.

Two ordering choices are worth explaining.

Sorted by confidence ascending. The claims the model was least sure of come first, because that is where a reviewer's attention is worth most. Sorting the other way would spend the scarcest resource in the system on the easiest decisions.

Retracted claims are excluded. A retraction cascade can withdraw a claim that was still sitting in the queue — the premise it rested on was retracted while it waited. Asking somebody to adjudicate something already withdrawn wastes their time, and worse, approving it would revive a withdrawn fact. Both the listing and the approve path filter on whether a claim is still current.

A bug the tests caught

Earlier, retracted assertions stayed in the queue and could be approved. The sequence: a cascade retracts a still-pending claim, a reviewer works through the queue, and approving it brings a withdrawn fact back into the live graph — with a fresh approval record attached, making it look more trustworthy than the facts around it. Both paths now check that a claim is current before acting.

What approving actually means

Approval is a named human act. The reviewer's identity is recorded, mandatory, along with the time and an optional note. There is deliberately no bulk-approve-everything operation in the system: a loop over the queue is one line of caller code, and at least it is a line somebody wrote on purpose.

Several transitions are refused, and each refusal is a design statement:

AttemptResultWhy
Approve something already approved Accepted, unchanged Idempotent. A double-click is not an error.
Approve something rejected Refused Re-extract rather than reviving a rejected claim, so the audit trail stays honest about what happened.
Approve something auto-asserted Refused It needs no approval, and approving it would imply a human checked something they did not.
Approve something retracted Refused Its basis was withdrawn. Approving would revive a withdrawn fact.
Reject something already live Refused Use retraction instead, so anything inferred from it is invalidated too.

The third row is the most unusual and the most characteristic. A system that let you approve auto-asserted facts would let a well-meaning administrator work through the whole graph adding signatures, and the signatures would mean nothing. Refusing the operation keeps the word "approved" meaning one specific thing.

Rejecting, and why the reason is mandatory

A rejection requires a reason. It is not bookkeeping: the reason is the training signal. It is the only place in the system where a human records why a model was wrong about a document, and that record is what makes it possible to improve extraction rather than merely filter it.

Rejected claims are never deleted. A rejected extraction is evidence about the extractor — that a particular version of a particular model, on a particular kind of passage, produced a claim a lawyer disagreed with. Deleting it discards the only data anybody has about how well extraction is working.

Staged and live are not the same as reviewed

Two questions are easy to conflate, and the difference matters when something goes wrong:

  1. Review state — has a person signed this off? Derived from how the fact was reached, and no code path can opt out.
  2. Staged or live — is this fact in the part of the graph queries read? A confirmed quote is signed off the moment it is created, but it still has to be written.

Staging is a transaction boundary. A whole document's claims land together or not at all, so a document that failed halfway through extraction contributes nothing rather than contributing a misleading subset. A half-ingested filing must never look like a complete one, because a lawyer reading a complete-looking filing draws conclusions from what is absent.

Promotion moves signed-off staged claims to live. Claims still awaiting review stay staged — that is the gate working, not a partial failure.

Retraction, and why it cascades

Withdrawing a fact is not deletion. It marks the fact superseded from a moment in time, and it transitively withdraws every conclusion that rested on it.

Withdrawing a fact. Anything inferred from it is withdrawn at the same time, so no conclusion outlives the reason it was drawn.

The failure this prevents is silent, which is why it is an enforced invariant rather than a convention. Retract the extraction that said counsel represents a party, leave the conflict flag standing, and the graph now asserts a conflict whose stated basis has been withdrawn. Every "why do you believe this?" answer for that flag cites a retracted premise — which is exactly the question the system exists to answer well, answered badly, on the highest-stakes fact in the graph.

Because nothing is deleted, a reading of the graph as of a date before the retraction still shows the fact and still shows the conflict flag it supported. That is not a quirk; it is the requirement. "What did the file show when we advised?" has to stay answerable after a correction, and that is what transaction time is for.

An audit record of one retraction keeps the root separate from the cascade, because they are different events to a reader: one person decided one thing, and a number of conclusions fell as a consequence. Collapsing them would misreport a single decision as many decisions.

Reviewing everything anyway

A firm that wants to review even the mechanically-confirmed claims can. A governance setting sends those to the queue as well, and the interface warns what will happen:

Pattern-matched facts now require review. The queue will grow considerably.

The reasonable use is onboarding — a firm building confidence in the extractors, for a period, with the intention of turning it off. Left on permanently it reintroduces exactly the queue-size problem the design exists to avoid, and the outcome is bulk approval.

Not built yet

That setting is stored, validated, and warned about when changed, but nothing reads it at extraction time. Turning it on currently changes nothing about what enters the queue. Review state is still derived purely from the epistemic class.

The same is true of the setting that would force review for governing relationships regardless of class: it is defined and documented, and no code consults it. Since the extractor already refuses to mark anything but a presence relationship as confirmed, every governing relationship from a document already goes to review by construction — so the setting is currently redundant rather than broken. It would matter if a system-of-record importer ever declared governing relationships directly.
