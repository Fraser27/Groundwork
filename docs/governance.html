Using it

Governance settings

Trust thresholds, kill switches, model choices and retrieval limits, set per firm and changeable without a redeployment. One relationship between two of them is enforced in code rather than merely documented, and it is the most important thing on this page.

src/governance.py · src/api/routes_governance.py

Why these are settings and not constants

Every value here could have been a constant in the source. Two reasons it is not.

A firm onboarding a new practice area will want a different confidence threshold from the one that suits its established work — more cautious in an unfamiliar area, and the caution has to be adjustable by the person accountable for it rather than by a developer.

And a model deprecation should be a settings change, not a release. When a model is retired, the alternative to a setting is an emergency deployment on somebody else's timetable.

Defaults come from the environment; overrides are stored per firm and survive a restart.

The cap/floor invariant

Two settings and the relationship between them:

SettingDefaultWhat it does
min_confidence_floor 0.80 Facts scoring below this do not inform answers. They stay visible in the review queue and the audit trail — this gates retrieval, not storage.
model_confidence_cap 0.79 The highest confidence any model-extracted interpretation may carry, regardless of what the model says about its own certainty.

The cap must stay strictly below the floor. That gap of 0.01 is the entire mechanism, and what it buys is this: an unreviewed model interpretation sits below the retrieval threshold by construction, so it cannot shape an answer even if the review gate were bypassed entirely.

Bypassed by what? By a bug. By a route written later that forgets to check. By a data import path nobody has thought of. The review gate is one mechanism; the confidence gap is a second, independent one, and both have to fail before an unapproved interpretation reaches a user.

The trap the invariant catches

An administrator decides the system is too cautious and lowers the floor from 0.80 to 0.70, to get answers to more questions.

The cap is still 0.79. The floor is now 0.70. Every unreviewed model interpretation in the graph — capped at 0.79, therefore above 0.70 — is suddenly eligible to shape answers. Nothing was approved. Nobody reviewed anything. One number moved, the review queue's entire purpose was silently voided, and no error occurred anywhere.

The system refuses the change. It returns an error explaining exactly why, in words meant to be shown to the administrator directly:

model_confidence_cap (0.79) must stay below min_confidence_floor (0.7). The gap is what keeps an unreviewed model assertion out of answers even if the review gate is bypassed; closing it removes that guarantee.

To genuinely lower the floor, the cap has to be lowered first, deliberately, as a separate act. The administrator has to state that they intend the consequence.

This is why the coupling is enforced in code rather than written in a runbook. A documented invariant is a hope. And critically, a rejected change does not half-apply: validation runs on a candidate copy of the settings, and a refusal leaves the live settings untouched rather than in a state where the floor moved and the cap did not.

The application's own explanation of the cap, shown next to the control:

The highest confidence an AI-extracted fact may claim. Kept just below the confidence floor on purpose, so an AI claim can never influence an answer until a person has approved it. The system will not let you raise this to or above the floor.

Trust thresholds

min_confidence_floor — default 0.80

How certain the system must be before a fact is used in an answer. Facts below it still appear in the review queue; they just do not influence results. Raising it makes answers more conservative and more likely to leave a question unanswered — which is often the correct outcome, and is always a better one than a confident wrong answer.

model_confidence_cap — default 0.79

Covered above. Note that a model's stated certainty is not treated as evidence at any point: the cap is applied to whatever it reports, and the extractor does not read the model's confidence for presence claims at all.

auto_assert_deterministic — default on

Whether mechanically-confirmed facts go live without review. Switching it off sends them to the queue too — slower, but a reasonable posture during onboarding while a firm builds trust in extraction.

Not enforced yet

Stored, validated, and warned about when changed. Nothing reads it at extraction time, so turning it off does not currently change what enters the queue.

require_review_for_governing — default on

Force review for the relationships that carry legal consequence — who represents whom, who is adverse to whom, privilege, deadlines — regardless of how the fact was reached. Belt and braces for the cases where a wrong answer is an exposure rather than an embarrassment.

Not enforced yet, and currently redundant

No code consults this setting. In practice it is redundant today: the extractor refuses to mark anything but a presence relationship as mechanically confirmed, so every governing relationship read from a document already goes to review by construction. It would begin to matter if a system-of-record importer ever declared governing relationships directly, since those would otherwise auto-assert.

Kill switches

block_ungoverned_queries — default off

Refuse tier-4 model-generated SQL entirely. Governed metrics and graph traversal keep working. Refusals are recorded — the firm, the user, the question, the reason, the time — so an administrator can see what people are trying to ask.

Treat that log as a backlog rather than an incident report. A question people keep asking is a governed metric waiting to be written. See the kill switch.

Fully wired. A firm that turns this on gets the refusal and the audit entry today, with a message written to be shown to a lawyer rather than a developer.

block_model_extraction — default off

Stop calling the model extractor. Documents still upload, parse, split and index for search, so their text remains findable; no facts are proposed from them.

Wired — ingest checks it and reports why extraction did not run.

Stale help text

The wording next to this control still says "Pattern-based extraction continues, so citations and docket numbers are still picked up", and the same claim appears in the setting's own code comment. That is left over from before the pattern-matching extractor was deleted. There is no pattern-based extraction any more, so switching this on stops all fact extraction from documents. The behaviour is correct; the explanation is out of date.

Models

Four model choices, each changeable without a release.

SettingUsed for
query_modelInterpreting questions and generating SQL.
extraction_modelReading documents and proposing facts.
enrichment_modelWriting descriptions for database tables and columns. A smaller, cheaper model is usually enough.
embedding_modelConverting document text into vectors for similarity search.

Changing the first three is routine, and safe because every fact records the specific versioned producer that created it. Old facts keep citing the model that made them; new ones cite the new model. The generations do not blur.

The embedding model is a data migration, not a setting

Changing it is not like changing the others. Existing vectors were produced by the previous model, and a similarity search across two generations of embeddings is not an error — it is silently meaningless. Nothing fails. Search quality degrades and no signal says so.

The interface warns and requires an explicit re-processing of every document. The warning is in capitals in the product's own help text, which is unusual for this codebase and deliberate.

The vector width is part of the same problem: a similarity search across two different dimensionalities is not an error either, for the same reason and with the same lack of symptom.

Vocabulary

ontology_domain — default legal

Which vocabulary of entities, relationships and rules this firm uses. The platform is domain-agnostic; the legal pack is simply the default. A healthcare pack ships alongside it.

enforce_closed_vocabulary — default on

Reject governing relationships the vocabulary does not define. The product's explanation is the clearest statement of the stakes anywhere in the interface:

Reject relationships the ontology does not define. This is what stops the same idea being recorded five different ways — if that happens, a conflict check can return no results and look like a clean report. Strongly recommended on.

Switching it off produces a warning to the same effect. See the conflict-check failure for the full worked example.

Not enforced yet

The setting is stored and warned about. The closed-vocabulary check itself is unconditional in the assertion builder — validation happens against the vocabulary the pack supplies, and no code path consults this flag to decide whether to skip it. In effect the protection is always on, which is the safe direction for the discrepancy to run.

Retrieval

vector_top_k — default 20

How many document passages to retrieve before expanding into the graph. Must be at least 1.

graph_expand_depth — default 2, range 1 to 5

How many relationship hops to follow out from a retrieved passage. Two is usually right; more pulls in weakly related matters. The range is enforced with a stated reason — deeper traversals fan out badly — and the number of paths grows roughly exponentially with depth, so a depth of 5 on a well-connected entity retrieves most of the firm.

Both are read by the resolver and genuinely take effect.

How a change is applied

A settings update is a partial patch, and the sequence is deliberate:

  1. Unknown field names are rejected outright. A typo in a setting name silently doing nothing is how a firm ends up believing a control is on when it is not.
  2. The patch is merged onto a candidate copy and validated there. A rejected change cannot leave the live settings half-updated.
  3. A refusal returns the validation message directly to the administrator. These messages are written to be read by a person accountable for the setting, not by a developer reading a log.
  4. Warnings are returned alongside a successful change where the change is safe but consequential — an embedding model swap, disabling the closed vocabulary, disabling auto-assertion.
  5. Who changed it and when are recorded on the settings themselves.

Governance changes require an administrator. That is checked separately from the tenant check, since a firm's ordinary users may read their firm's settings without being able to change them.

A test that keeps the help text readable

Every setting has plain-language help text written for a lawyer-administrator rather than a data engineer — "will not be used in answers" rather than "excluded from the retrieval candidate set".

There is a test asserting that no jargon leaks into it. That is an unusual thing to test, and it is there because help text decays: the person who understands a setting best is the person least able to notice that their explanation of it has become incomprehensible. A test is the only thing that holds the line.

These documents reuse that wording deliberately, so the tooltip in the product and the explanation here cannot drift apart.
